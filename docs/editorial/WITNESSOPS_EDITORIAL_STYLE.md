# WitnessOps editorial style

Scope: public essays, research notes, evidence reconstructions and field notes. Use alongside [ARTICLE_CONTRACT.md](./ARTICLE_CONTRACT.md). This guides drafting and review; it does not authorize publication or turn prose into verification authority.

## Voice

Write as a technically curious operator trying to understand what is actually true. Be direct, technically literate, skeptical and comfortable with uncertainty. Investigate mechanisms, evidence, incentives and failure modes. Do not presume fraud, conspiracy, incompetence or malice. Follow a mechanism one or two consequences beyond its obvious interpretation.

Prefer simple words, concrete systems and examples. Avoid corporate marketing, generic consulting language, academic performance and management abstractions that conceal the mechanism.

## Argument and rhythm

A useful argument often starts with the common interpretation, uncovers a quieter mechanism, tests it through a concrete example, follows its consequences, asks what happens at scale, marks uncertainty and ends with a practical question. Let the reader follow the argument being discovered. These are reasoning prompts, not seven required headings.

Use short and medium sentences. Occasional very short paragraphs can carry weight; do not give every sentence its own paragraph. Longer sentences are appropriate when the reasoning needs them. Use emphasis sparingly and keep long-form reading comfortable.

Avoid stock openings and transitions such as “In today’s rapidly evolving landscape”, “As organizations navigate”, “It is important to note”, “This underscores the critical importance” and “At its core”. Avoid repetitive “It is not X, it is Y” framing, generic rhetorical triples, inflated conclusions, fake gravitas and automatic sales-CTA endings. Do not globally ban useful ordinary words because models sometimes overuse them.

## Author identity boundary

An agent may reproduce the editorial style, never fabricate the author's experience. Do not invent things Karol witnessed, conversations he had, customers he served, experiments he performed, opinions or political beliefs, quotations, or unsupported first-person factual claims. Author observations must be explicitly supplied by the author. If required material is missing, write neutrally or mark it as requiring author input. Plausible prose is not evidence.

## Pre-publication self-check

- What is the central question in one sentence?
- What is the strongest factual claim, and what evidence supports it?
- Which parts are inference rather than direct observation?
- What remains unknown?
- Did I invent any author experience, quote, experiment or opinion?
- Can a technically competent reader understand the mechanism?
- Is any paragraph present mainly to sound impressive?
- Can unnecessary words be removed without losing meaning?
- Does the ending leave a sharper question, mechanism or practical consequence?

This is a drafting/review aid, not an application workflow or permission to publish.
